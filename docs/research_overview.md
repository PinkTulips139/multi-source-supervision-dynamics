# Research Overview

## Background

Synthetic data is increasingly used to expand training sets, reduce data collection cost, and adapt models to new domains. In recursive training, later-generation models are trained partly or fully on data generated by earlier models. This process can be useful, but it also introduces risks.

## Risks in Recursive Synthetic Training

Recursive use of generated data may cause:

- Loss of low-probability modes
- Weakening of long-tail coverage
- Error inheritance across generations
- Error amplification through repeated training
- Distribution drift away from real data

The project treats these as risks to be empirically tested, not as guaranteed outcomes in every setting.

## Why Source Count Is Not Enough

A workflow may use several generators, but those generators may not provide independent information. They may share:

- The same base model family
- Similar pretraining data
- Similar instruction tuning data
- Similar decoding behavior
- Similar knowledge boundaries
- Similar failure modes

Therefore, the number of sources is only a nominal measure of diversity. Effective diversity should also consider whether the generators fail on the same examples.

## Why Study Error Correlation

If generators make errors on different samples, their outputs may be complementary. If they make errors on the same samples, mixing them may reinforce shared mistakes. Error correlation is a way to describe this shared failure structure.

The working assumption is that lower correlated errors may provide more useful source diversity for recursive training, but this remains to be tested.

## Current Research Boundary

This repository currently focuses on research design, literature mapping, and experiment planning. It does not yet provide final algorithms or validated empirical conclusions.

Planned experiment settings include:

1. Controlled Gaussian mixture simulations
2. Real text classification tasks such as IMDb, SST-2, and AG News

## Validation Status

The proposed method and hypotheses have not yet been empirically validated.
